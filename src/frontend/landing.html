The problem

Agents can do the work. Supervising one still takes hands.

AI coding agents browse, edit files and run commands. Before anything risky they stop and ask: Allow?

Someone who can't type, click or speak can't answer. So they can't safely use an agent at all.

People who can think and decide but can't press a key: for example, people living with ALS or a spinal cord injury.

Our solution

Three signals. One job each.

Silence

Safe steps go ahead after a visible six-second countdown. Do nothing and the work continues.

Close your eyes

The next action is refused. A webcam sees it and your alpha brainwaves confirm it: two independent brakes.

Bite down

Commits, deletes, downloads, web lookups and anything not on a short safe list wait. Only a short bite releases them.

    The agentwriting code, driving a sandboxed browser
    • Risk is default-deny. No countdown can approve a risky step. Only a bite.
    • Take the band off and the agent stops. So does losing the connection.
    • Closing the window never lifts a stop. The brake and help work without the network.

    Impact

    Accessibility is moving from operating a computer to supervising an agent.

    Supervising takes a handful of decisions, not hundreds of keystrokes. A few reliable body signals can carry that. rein shows it with a headband and a camera. The wearer touches no keyboard after the mission starts.

    How to use it

    Four steps, then your hands stay in your lap.

    1. 1Wet and wearDamp the Muse's sensors, seat it snug, and press its button.
    2. 2Connect and calibratePick the Muse in rein. Read the screen for 20 seconds, eyes open, jaw loose.
    3. 3Start the agentRun your agent with the rein plugin and give it its mission.
    4. 4SuperviseSay nothing to continue. Close your eyes to stop. Bite to approve a risky step.

    Routines start a saved mission with one bite, so there's nothing to type. Stats for nerds, one button in the app, shows the live signal quality, brainwaves and the pulse, so you can see why it decided.
